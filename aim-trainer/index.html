    Improving, by the evidence

    How motor skills actually get better

    • Short daily sessions beat long weekly ones.Spaced practice is one of the most reliable findings in motor learning: the same total minutes spread over several days are retained better than one long block. Aim for 15 to 20 focused minutes a day.
    • Sleep is part of training.Motor skills consolidate during sleep, so it's normal to score higher the next day than at the end of a session. Don't chase a personal best when you're tired; stop and come back tomorrow.
    • Train where you fail some of the time.The challenge point framework says learning is fastest when a task is hard enough that you miss regularly. That's why the ladder moves you up the moment you pass instead of letting you farm an easy tier.
    • Mix your drills.Interleaving different tasks (contextual interference) lowers your score during a session but improves what you keep and how well it carries into real games. Rotate tracking with the Doomfist drills rather than doing 10 runs of one.
    • Watch the target, not your hand.Research on attentional focus finds that an external focus (keep the crosshair on the target's chest or head) learns faster than thinking about wrist or arm movement. Check your grip and posture between runs, not during them.
    • Keep your sensitivity fixed.Your brain is learning a mapping from hand distance to turn angle. Every sens change resets part of it. Once you've settled on a sens (2.26 fits your pad), stay on it for at least a few months unless something is clearly wrong.
    • Expect a slow first run.The first runs of a day are lower (warm-up decrement). Judge yourself on the week's trend in the history chart, not on single runs. Flat stretches of one to two weeks are normal before a jump.
    • Carry it into real games.Trainers build raw control; games teach timing and target choice. After your session, play a few minutes of Doomfist in a custom game or deathmatch and use the same focus.

    Background: Schmidt and Lee, Motor Control and Learning; Guadagnoli and Lee (2004) on the challenge point framework; Wulf (2013) on attentional focus; Walker et al. (2002) on sleep and motor consolidation.

    The drills

    What each one trains

    Rocket PunchCharge while tracking a moving enemy, then release only when you're on them. Most punches miss because the aim drifts during the charge, not because of the release. Hold any mouse button to charge.
    Hand Cannon rangeDoomfist fights at a few metres, where a strafing enemy crosses your screen far faster than at range. Same tracking skill, much higher angular speed, bigger target and more jumps.
    Slam & switch flicksSeismic Slam is aimed at the ground under enemies, and diving the backline means snapping between targets. Orbs sit mostly below the horizon at wide angles. Score is hits minus half your misses, so clicking blindly costs you.
    Flick frenzyPure target acquisition: one orb at a time, anywhere on screen, on a timer that shortens each tier. Trains reacting and snapping fast without overshooting, which is what landing a punch on a fleeing enemy needs.
    Hazard one-shotThe slash-into-shot kill (slash 70, then one Bonespur volley; with the Leap perk the shot fires a free extra shot at 65%). The slash knocks the target back; you flick to the head and fire one Bonespur volley before they recover. Pellet impacts show where each of the 10 landed. Up close, the centre pellet and the 1.8° ring fit on a head; the 3.5° corners usually don't, so most kills come from centring the head perfectly.
    Sens finderThe PSA method: 7 rounds comparing a lower and a higher sensitivity on the tracking drill, halving the gap toward whichever felt better. Round 1 tries half and one and a half times your current sens, so a bad starting point gets corrected fast. Run it on three different days and use the average.
